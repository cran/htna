Many interactions in education and work are carried out by actors of different kinds: a learner and a tutor, a student and a generative AI assistant, a programmer and a coding agent. A record of such an interaction is a sequence of coded events in which the actors take turns, and the questions it raises concern both what each actor does and how the actors respond to each other. Transition network analysis (TNA) models a sequence of events as a network whose nodes are the codes and whose weighted, directed edges are the probabilities of moving from one code to the next. Applied to a mixed sequence, ordinary TNA treats all codes alike and loses the information that some codes belong to one actor and some to another.

Heterogeneous transition network analysis (HTNA) keeps that information. Each code is assigned to one actor type, the network is estimated over the interleaved sequence so that transitions within and between actors are counted from the same events, and the actor partition is carried through every subsequent step: the layout and colours of the plot, the summaries of transitions within and between actors, and the patterns of turn-taking (López-Pernas, Misiejuk, & Saqr, 2026). This vignette sets out the formulation and follows one analysis from data to assessed conclusions.

library(htna)

The model

A session is a sequence of coded events \(c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_T\), and every code \(i\) belongs to exactly one actor type \(a(i)\), for example Human or AI. Counting, over all sessions, how often code \(i\) is directly followed by code \(j\) gives the counts \(n_{ij}\). The relative transition network has the weights

\[w_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{\sum_k n_{ik}},\]

the estimated probability that an event coded \(i\) is followed by an event coded \(j\). The actor partition divides the weight matrix \(W\) into blocks \(W_{AB}\), holding the transitions from codes of actor \(A\) to codes of actor \(B\). The diagonal blocks (\(A = B\)) describe how an actor continues its own turn, and the off-diagonal blocks describe the handovers between actors. Because each row of \(W\) sums to one, the row sum of code \(i\) within block \(W_{AB}\), \(\sum_{j:\,a(j) = B} w_{ij}\), is the probability that an event coded \(i\) is followed by an event of actor \(B\). A meta-path is the sequence of actor types that a path of codes passes through, such as Human \(\to\) AI \(\to\) Human.

Data

human_ai holds the coded turns of 429 sessions in which programmers worked with an AI coding agent (“vibe coding”), from 34 projects. Each row is one coded event; a message can carry several codes.

  • Session: session_id identifies the sequence; transitions are counted within sessions.
  • Order: order_in_session orders the events within a session.
  • Code: code is the event code, six for the human (Check, Frustrate, Inquire, Refine, Request, Specify) and six for the AI (Ask, Delegate, Execute, Plan, Repair, Report).
  • Actor type: actor_type ("Human" or "AI") assigns each event to its actor.
  • Phase: phase (Early, Late) splits the sessions chronologically in half; it is used in the grouped vignette.
with(human_ai, table(actor_type))
#> actor_type
#>    AI Human 
#>  8551 10796

The 19,347 events consist of 10,796 human and 8,551 AI events.

The network plot

plot_htna() draws the network with the codes of each actor on their own arc, in the actor’s colour and shape; edges below 0.05 are not drawn.

plot_htna(net)

Edge width is proportional to the weight, and only the 73 edges with a weight of at least 0.05 are drawn (the same edges that the pruning step below keeps), so the plot shows the transitions that carry the interaction.

Which codes are central

Centralities rank the codes by their position in the network. In-strength is the total transition weight flowing into a code, out-strength the weight leaving it, and betweenness the number of shortest paths through it.

centralities_htna(net, measures = c("InStrength", "OutStrength",
                                    "Betweenness"))

Execute receives the most transition weight (in-strength 2.03) and lies on the most shortest paths (betweenness 29), followed on in-strength by the human Request (1.69). The out-strengths are all close to one because each row of a relative network sums to one, less any self-transition. The interaction is organized around the request-and-execute exchange.

Focusing on the strong transitions

Pruning sets weak edges aside so that the structure carried by the strong transitions can be read.

pruned <- prune_htna(net, threshold = 0.05)
as.data.frame(summary(pruned), what = "actor_pairs")
#>   source_actor target_actor n_edges
#> 1        Human        Human      19
#> 2        Human           AI      14
#> 3           AI        Human      27
#> 4           AI           AI      13

At a threshold of 0.05, 73 edges remain. The AI-to-human block keeps 27 of its 36 edges, more than any other block, while the AI-to-AI block keeps 13: after an AI action the next event is most often a human one.

Turn-taking patterns

The alternating meta-path Human \(\to\) AI \(\to\) Human is the most frequent (3,593 occurrences in 402 sessions) and occurs 1.41 times as often as the frequencies of the two actors would predict. Runs within one actor are rare by the same standard: three consecutive human events have a lift of 0.46 and three AI events of 0.25. The interaction is a dialogue of short turns.

The sessions over time

The network pools the transitions of all sessions and positions. A sequence plot shows the sessions themselves, one row per session and actor, with each cell coloured by its code.

sequence_plot_htna(net, type = "heatmap")

How certain are the edges

The bootstrap resamples the sessions and re-estimates the network to show which edge weights the data determine precisely.

boot <- bootstrap_htna(net, iter = 100, seed = 1)
boot
#>   Edge                   Mean     95% CI          p
#>   -----------------------------------------------
#>   Delegate → Plan        0.609  [0.541, 0.670]  ** 
#>   Refine → Specify       0.412  [0.360, 0.468]  ** 
#>   Ask → Plan             0.411  [0.384, 0.437]  ** 
#>   Check → Execute        0.411  [0.382, 0.444]  ** 
#>   Repair → Execute       0.389  [0.333, 0.454]  ** 
#>   ... and 55 more significant edges
#> 
#> Bootstrap Network  [Transition Network (relative) | directed]
#>   Iterations : 100  |  Nodes : 12
#>   Edges      : 60 significant / 135 total
#>   CI         : 95%  |  Inference: stability  |  CR [0.75, 1.25]

With 100 resamples, 60 of the 135 edges are reproduced within the consistency range and marked significant; the strongest, Delegate to Plan, has a mean weight of 0.609 with a 95% interval from 0.541 to 0.670. A real analysis would use the default 1,000 resamples. The plot draws the non-significant edges dashed.

plot_htna_bootstrap(boot)

Are the conclusions stable

Two checks ask whether the conclusions depend on the particular sessions observed. Centrality stability drops increasing shares of the sessions and correlates the centralities with those of the full network (Epskamp, Borsboom, & Fried, 2018).

cs <- centrality_stability_htna(net, iter = 100, seed = 1)
as.data.frame(cs)
#>       measure  cs
#> 1  InStrength 0.9
#> 2 OutStrength 0.9
#> 3 Betweenness 0.9

All three measures keep a correlation above 0.7 until 90% of the sessions are dropped (CS coefficient 0.90), well above the recommended 0.5, so the centrality ranking can be reported. Split-half reliability estimates the network from two random halves of the sessions and compares them.

rel <- reliability_htna(net, iter = 100, seed = 1)
summary(rel)
#>      model     metric   mean       sd
#> 1 relative   mean_dev 0.0125 0.001146
#> 2 relative median_dev 0.0075 0.000883
#> 3 relative        cor 0.9820 0.004916
#> 4 relative    max_dev 0.0982 0.031284

The edge weights of the two halves correlate at 0.982 on average and differ by 0.0125 on average, so the estimated structure does not depend on which sessions happened to be observed.
