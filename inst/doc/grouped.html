Many questions about interactions between actors are comparative. Does the way students work with an AI assistant change over a course? Do teams in an experimental condition hand work to the AI differently from teams in a control condition? Answering them requires one heterogeneous transition network per group of sessions, estimated over the same codes and the same actor partition so that the networks can be set side by side, described with the same measures and assessed with the same procedures.

A grouped heterogeneous transition network is such a set: one network per cohort, each built from the sessions of that cohort, all sharing the actor types. build_htna() creates it from a session-level grouping column, and the other functions of the package accept the grouped network as it is and work cohort by cohort (or, for comparisons, pair by pair). This vignette builds, describes and assesses a grouped network of early and late sessions; the comparison of networks is treated in depth in the guide Comparing networks.

library(htna)

Data

human_ai holds the coded turns of 429 sessions in which programmers worked with an AI coding agent. The variables used here are:

  • Session and order: session_id, order_in_session.
  • Code and actor type: code (six human and six AI codes) and actor_type ("Human", "AI").
  • Cohort: phase, a factor with levels Early and Late: the sessions ordered by their first date and split in half.
xtabs(~ phase, data = unique(subset(human_ai, select = c(session_id, phase))))
#> phase
#> Early  Late 
#>   214   215
xtabs(~ phase, data = human_ai)
#> phase
#> Early  Late 
#> 10170  9177

The early cohort has 214 sessions with 10,170 coded events and the late cohort 215 sessions with 9,177 events.

One network per cohort

Passing the name of the cohort column as group gives one network per cohort, in the order of the factor levels.

grp <- build_htna(human_ai, actor_type = "actor_type", group = "phase")
grp
#> Group Networks (2 groups)
#> 
#>   Group  Nodes  Edges  Weights
#>   Early  12     135    [0.001, 0.689]
#>   Late   12     134    [0.001, 0.508]
as.data.frame(summary(grp), what = "actor_pairs")
#>   group source_actor target_actor n_edges
#> 1 Early        Human        Human      35
#> 2 Early        Human           AI      36
#> 3 Early           AI        Human      36
#> 4 Early           AI           AI      28
#> 5  Late        Human        Human      35
#> 6  Late        Human           AI      36
#> 7  Late           AI        Human      36
#> 8  Late           AI           AI      27

Both cohorts use all twelve codes, and their non-zero edges fall in the actor-pair blocks in almost the same way: the only difference is one AI to AI transition that occurs in the early sessions (28 edges) but not in the late ones (27). The cohorts differ, if at all, in the weights of the transitions rather than in which transitions occur.

Assessing each cohort

Each cohort is estimated from about half of the sessions, so its edges are less certain than those of the pooled network. The bootstrap of a grouped network resamples the sessions of each cohort separately.

boots <- bootstrap_htna(grp, iter = 100, seed = 1)

With 100 resamples, 41 of the 135 early edges and 39 of the 134 late edges are reproduced within the consistency range. The strongest edge in both cohorts, Delegate to Plan, has a mean weight of 0.689 in the early sessions and 0.518 in the late ones, with intervals (0.624 to 0.781 and 0.419 to 0.604) that do not overlap. summary(boots) returns the edges of both cohorts in one table. The plots keep only the significant edges.

op <- par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
plot_htna_bootstrap(boots, display = "significant")

par(op)

Centrality stability is computed per cohort in the same way.

stab <- centrality_stability_htna(grp, iter = 100, seed = 1)
as.data.frame(stab)
#>   group     measure  cs
#> 1 Early  InStrength 0.9
#> 2 Early OutStrength 0.8
#> 3 Early Betweenness 0.8
#> 4  Late  InStrength 0.9
#> 5  Late OutStrength 0.9
#> 6  Late Betweenness 0.8

Every coefficient is 0.8 or 0.9, above the recommended 0.5 (Epskamp, Borsboom, & Fried, 2018), so the centralities of each cohort can be interpreted and compared.

A first comparison

The descriptive comparison summarizes how the cohorts differ, overall and within each actor-pair block.

compare_htna(grp)
#> HTNA network comparisons: 1 cohort pair
#> 
#> --- Early vs Late ---
#>   Pearson correlation of edge weights: 0.959
#>   Mean absolute difference:            0.0192
#>  source target cells changed mean_diff mean_abs_diff max_abs_diff
#>   Human  Human    36      35   0.00168        0.0159       0.0631
#>   Human     AI    36      36  -0.00168        0.0179       0.0922
#>      AI  Human    36      36  -0.00622        0.0216       0.0571
#>      AI     AI    36      28   0.00622        0.0212       0.1810

The edge weights of the two cohorts correlate at 0.959 and differ by 0.019 on average. The largest single difference, 0.181, lies in the AI-to-AI block. A permutation test asks which differences exceed what reshuffling the sessions between the cohorts produces.

perm <- permutation_htna(grp, iter = 200, seed = 1)
perm
#> Grouped Permutation Test
#> Groups: Early vs Late 
#> 
#> -- Early vs Late --
#> Permutation Test: Transition Network (relative probabilities) [directed]
#>   Iterations: 200  |  Alpha: 0.05
#>   Nodes: 12  |  Edges tested: 135  |  Significant: 28
#>   Global test (networks differ overall?): M = 2.760 (p = 0.00498)  |  S = 0.181 (p = 0.0199)

With 200 permutations, 28 of the 135 edges differ significantly, and the global test rejects equal networks (p = 0.005 for the sum of absolute differences). The difference network draws the significant edges, green where the early cohort is higher and red where the late cohort is.

plot_htna_diff(perm)

The guide Comparing networks adds the Bayesian comparison and the full comparison tables, and the case study early and late sessions of vibe coding interprets the differences.

Working with the grouped object

Every function of the package accepts the grouped network as it is: summary(), plot_htna(), centralities_htna(), sequence_plot_htna(), bootstrap_htna(), centrality_stability_htna() and casedrop_reliability_htna() work cohort by cohort; compare_htna(), permutation_htna(), bayes_compare_htna() and sequence_compare_htna() work on the pairs of cohorts. A single cohort is analyzed on its own by building it from its sessions, for example build_htna(subset(human_ai, phase == "Early"), actor_type = "actor_type").
